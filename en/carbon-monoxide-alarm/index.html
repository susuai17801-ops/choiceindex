How to Choose a Carbon Monoxide Alarm in Taiwan: the Approval Standard, Type Approval Number, Service Life, and Rental Water Heaters (一氧化碳警報器)

Last updated: October 25, 2026

Many renters in Taiwan use gas water heaters in winter and search Shopee for a carbon monoxide alarm. Prices range from a few dozen to over a thousand NT dollars, and the product page usually says only “國際認證” (international certification), so it is hard to tell the listings apart. We checked official Taiwanese sources: the Ministry of the Interior has an approval standard for carbon monoxide alarms and gas leak alarms that sets detection levels and times, alarm volume, the low-battery notice, and labeling on the unit, while the National Fire Agency (NFA) focuses its guidance on the type and location of water heaters. We did not find a buying guide for alarms from the NFA or the BSMI, and we did not find a comparison test. This page summarizes the official material we could find, plus a checklist before ordering on Shopee.

First, the Types of Alarm

TypeWhat it detects (per the approval standard)What to watch for
Carbon monoxide alarmThe standard's scope includes alarms for carbon monoxide produced by incomplete combustionA gas water heater with poor exhaust produces carbon monoxide, and this type of alarm detects the carbon monoxide itself
Gas leak alarmThe standard's scope includes alarms for leaks of natural gas or LPGIt detects leaking gas, not carbon monoxide, so the two cannot replace each other
Combination typeThe standard lists carbon monoxide, natural gas, LPG, and combination types as separate kindsRead the product name closely: carbon monoxide only, or both

The standard also divides devices by place of use: household, commercial, and dual household-commercial. There is no “best” type. The right choice depends on whether you want to detect carbon monoxide, gas leaks, or both. Smoke (fire) alarms detect something different and are not covered here. The types above come from the approval standard, and this page only summarizes them. It is not product safety advice.

Beyond the Alarm: What Matters for Rental Water Heaters (per the NFA)

  • Outdoor-type heaters belong outdoors with good ventilation — The NFA says outdoor-type water heaters should be installed only outdoors in a well-ventilated place; indoors or in poorly ventilated places, switch to a forced-exhaust heater that vents exhaust outside, installed by a qualified installer (NFA, “5 principles for installing water heaters”, December 16, 2021).
  • Wrong installation locations — The NFA's rental water heater guide lists wrong places for an outdoor-type heater: a balcony enclosed with windows, a stairwell, a kitchen, and a bathroom. Fixes: move it outdoors, switch to a forced-exhaust heater, or switch to an electric water heater. This guide file carries no date.
  • Qualified installers and the work label — The guide says gas water heaters should be installed according to law by a qualified installer and carry a work label; installer lists can be looked up on local fire department websites.
  • Landlords should provide a correctly installed heater — The guide reminds landlords to provide a correctly installed water heater so tenants are not poisoned by carbon monoxide.
  • An alarm does not replace correct installation — Both NFA documents focus on the type and location of the water heater and do not say an alarm can replace them. We do not give safety advice, so for a rental, follow the NFA and your local fire department.

Before You Order, Confirm These

  • Confirm whether you want to guard against carbon monoxide, gas leaks, or both, and that the product name says so.
  • Check whether the product page shows a type approval number; if not, ask the seller, and check the label on the unit when it arrives.
  • Ask the seller about the sensor's service life and how long one battery lasts; if the page does not say, you need to ask.
  • If the store offers a first-generation and a second-generation type, ask what the difference is before choosing.
  • Read the warranty coverage and return rules, and save screenshots of the store page.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can a carbon monoxide alarm replace a correctly installed water heater?
The NFA's guidance focuses on the type and location of the water heater: outdoor-type heaters go outdoors with good ventilation, and indoors or in poorly ventilated places a forced-exhaust type installed by a qualified installer is needed. The documents we read do not say an alarm can replace this. We do not give safety advice, so follow the NFA and your local fire department.
Is the Ministry of the Interior's approval standard mandatory?
The standard's page does not say whether it is mandatory for homes, and we did not find out whether a product like this must have an approval number, so we cannot say a product without one is illegal. What we suggest is to check the product page and the unit when it arrives for labels such as the type approval number. This standard was issued on December 26, 2008 (year 97 of the Republic of China calendar), and the page lists no revision date, so it may not be the latest version.
What is the difference between the first and second generation?
The product page describes the second generation as “探頭、零件升級” (upgraded probe and parts) but does not say what was upgraded. The product page does not explain the difference, so ask the seller before ordering.
Where should it be installed? How long does the sensor last?
The NFA documents and approval standard we read do not say where an alarm should be installed and do not set a number of years of service life (they only ask for a stated service life). This product page also does not give the sensor's life. Install it according to the instruction manual and confirm the service life with the seller.
What if I feel dizzy or nauseous?
Follow the NFA and local fire department guidance, and call 119 if needed. We do not give medical or safety advice.
