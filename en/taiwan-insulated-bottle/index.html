Insulated Bottles (Thermos) in Taiwan: How to Read Heat Retention, 304 Stainless Steel Labels, and Size (保溫瓶)

Last updated: November 27, 2026

Whether you want to carry hot water or tea on a winter day or iced water in summer, almost everyone in Taiwan has an insulated bottle (保溫瓶 / 保溫杯). But “which brand keeps heat best” is hard to answer in one sentence. A more practical approach is to read the heat retention label and the steel label on the product, then pick a size for how you will use it. This page explains how to read those labels, then recommends one small and one large bottle.

How to Read Heat Retention: “How Many Degrees After How Many Hours”

Brand-name insulated bottles often list “heat retention: ○○℃ or above after 6 hours” and “cold retention: ○○℃ or below after 6 hours”. The higher the hot-temperature reading (or the lower the cold-temperature reading), the better the bottle retains heat or cold under the seller's test conditions. For example, Zojirushi Taiwan's official site gives the test method for the SU-DA80: hot water is added at a room temperature of 20±2℃, and once it cools to 95±1℃ the bottle stands for 1 and 6 hours before the water temperature is measured just below the neck; when comparing, also check whether the test conditions are listed.

How to Read Stainless Steel Labels

  • Steel grade name — The labeling guideline for stainless steel food containers from Taiwan's Food and Drug Administration (食藥署) recommends listing the steel grade, ideally with the standard and grade, such as “JIS SUS304”, “AISI 304”, or “CNS 304”. If it only says “stainless steel” or “premium stainless steel”, you cannot tell which kind it is.
  • 300 series is common for tableware — According to Taiwan's FDA, stainless steel falls roughly into the 200, 300, and 400 series: the 200 series is high in manganese and less rust-resistant, so it is rarely used for tableware; most tableware uses the 300 series (such as 304); the 400 series is magnetic and is mostly used for knives.
  • Country of origin, production date, maker — The guideline also recommends listing the country of origin, production date, and manufacturer details. A 2014 news release from Taiwan's Ministry of Health and Welfare said that of 49 stainless steel food containers tested, none contained arsenic, lead, or cadmium, but 19 had non-compliant labels (missing origin, production date, and so on).
  • Normal use is not a big worry — According to Taiwan's FDA, stainless steel resists heat and acid corrosion, so under normal use it does not release harmful substances when exposed to heat, cold, acids, or alkalis; if the surface is badly dented, scaled, or rusty, it is best to replace it.

How to Choose a Size

  • About 360 ml — Light and easy to fit in a bag, good for one hot drink on your commute; the seller lists our small pick at about 0.17 kg.
  • About 800 ml — For a full day out or for drinking more water at work; the seller lists our large pick at about 0.36 kg, and filling the bottle adds about 0.8 kg to its weight (our rough figure).
  • Lid — A flip-top lid opens with one hand, good for drinking on the go; a screw lid or a lid with a carry loop is easier to carry and seals tighter.

Using and Cleaning

  • Wash a new bottle first: Taiwan's FDA guideline suggests washing with a neutral dish detergent, rinsing well, then filling it 80–90% with hot water and rinsing twice more.
  • Follow the manual on what drinks it can hold and whether it can go in a dishwasher; the seller says our large pick can hold sports drinks and is dishwasher-safe, but we did not find this information for the small pick.
  • Take the center plug and seal apart to wash them, and let them dry before putting them back.
  • If the surface is badly dented, scaled, or rusty, Taiwan's FDA suggests replacing it.

Frequently Asked Questions

Taiwan's FDA guideline recommends listing the steel grade, such as SUS304 or CNS 304. If it only says “stainless steel” or “premium stainless steel”, you cannot tell the grade; ask the seller first.
