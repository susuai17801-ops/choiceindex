How to Choose Kitchen Sponges and Scouring Pads (海綿菜瓜布) in Taiwan: Pot and Stove Type, Delicate Dish Type, and Materials

Last updated: October 23, 2026

A pot that stays dirty after scrubbing, a cup you are afraid of scratching, and a scouring pad that starts to smell after a short time are often signs that you are using the wrong material. On the shop page the names for sponge, scouring pad, steel wool, and delicate type are many, and claims such as "super stain removal", "does not hurt tableware", and "antibacterial" sound attractive, but each type suits different things to wash, and how rough the scrubbing side is also differs a lot. This guide explains the 4 materials, 4 things to check, and what to remember when using and caring for them, so you can decide which one to buy.

First, Understand the 4 Common Materials of Kitchen Sponges and Scouring Pads

TypeFeaturesWho it is for
Sponge plus scouring pad (two layers)One side is a sponge and the other is a scouring fiber layer. The sponge layer soaks up water and lathers easily, and the scouring layer scrubs off dirt, so one piece can do both bowls and pots. How rough and hard the fiber side is depends on the style, and they are commonly split into a rougher pot type and a more delicate tableware typeHomes that wash ordinary dishes and pots and want to handle most cleaning with one item
Delicate scouring padSofter and more durable. Some are labeled as containing no abrasive grit or corundum grit, and they are usually used on cups, dishes, and glassware. The cleaning power is gentler, so you need to scrub a few more times on thick burnt-on greasePeople who often wash teacups, glasses, and porcelain and are afraid of scratches
Coarse fiber, steel woolStrong scrubbing power, suited to removing burnt-on black marks and stubborn grease from iron pans, grills, and stoves. But they easily leave scratches on surfaces such as non-stick coatings, enamel, and polished stainless steelPeople who often fry, cook, or grill, and have iron pans or grills to clean
Plain sponge, microfiber clothGood at soaking up water and lathering, and gentler on surfaces, suited to glass, plastic, non-stick, and coated cookware. They are usually not strong enough for dried-on burnt residue or thick greasePeople with non-stick or coated cookware, or who mainly wash cups and dishes

No single type is "the best". It depends on what you are washing, whether the surface has a coating, and how often you wash. A common approach in ordinary homes is to use a rougher type for pots, a delicate type for dishes and cups, and a soft sponge for coated cookware, and keeping them separate makes scratches less likely. This site only explains how to choose and is not product safety advice. Before you buy, go by the scope of use stated on the pack and the listing.

4 Things to Check When Choosing Kitchen Sponges and Scouring Pads

What to checkWhat to look at
The items and surfaces it goes withFirst work out what you mainly wash. Iron pans, stainless steel pots, grills, and stoves can take a rougher type. Teacups, glasses, and porcelain tableware need a delicate type. For non-stick and coated cookware, enamel, and polished surfaces, avoid coarse types and use a soft sponge or a type that says it is suitable
How rough the surface is, and the labelLook at whether the pack or listing says "for pots and stoves" or "for delicate tableware", and whether it says no abrasive grit or corundum grit. Some brands use colors to show how rough it is, but the meaning of a color differs between brands, so go by the text on the pack and not only the color
Durability, antibacterial claims, and careWhether the sponge layer and scouring layer are bonded firmly, and whether the pad separates into layers or sheds fibers with use. Claims on the listing such as "durable and does not separate easily" and "antibacterial" are mostly the seller's or maker's description, and we have not tested them. Even if it says antibacterial, wringing it out after use and drying it in a ventilated place usually matters more for hygiene than the label
Count, and using them separatelyA bulk pack suits using separate pieces for pots, dishes, and other areas, so you do not wash everything with one piece. Before you buy, divide the total price by the number of pieces to get the cost per piece, and think about how many you use in a month so you do not buy too many and let them get old

What to Remember When Using and Caring for Them

  • Test on a small area the first time you use it on a new item — If you are not sure of an item's material or whether it has a coating, try a light scrub in an inconspicuous spot first, and only clean the whole thing once you are sure it leaves no scratches.
  • Do not use rough types on non-stick, coated cookware, or surfaces that scratch easily — Pot-type scouring pads usually scrub harder and easily scratch coatings, enamel, and polished surfaces. For these items, use a soft sponge or a type that the pack says is suitable.
  • Do not use them to clean people or food — A kitchen scouring pad is a cleaning product for utensils, and listings mostly say it must not be used on people or food. Use it as the pack says.
  • Rinse, wring out, and dry in a ventilated place after use — If it sits in water for a long time or stays in a damp corner, it is more likely to develop a smell and bacteria. Wringing it out and putting it where air can move matters more than anything else.
  • Replace it when it is dirty, damaged, or smells, and do not keep using it — There is no standard replacement time, and it depends on how often you use it and its condition. If you see a smell, shedding, damage, or it still feels sticky after washing, replace it. Use separate pieces for utensils used for raw and cooked food, and for areas outside the kitchen.

Frequently Asked Questions

Will a scouring pad scratch a non-stick pan?
It can. A rougher fiber side or steel wool can easily leave scratches on non-stick coatings, enamel, and polished stainless steel when you scrub hard, and once the coating is damaged it can affect use. For non-stick pans, use a soft sponge or a type that the pack says is suitable, and go by the cookware's own instructions.
How often should I replace a sponge or scouring pad?
There is no standard, and it depends on how often you use it, how greasy the things you wash are, and whether you dry it properly. A common suggestion is to replace it regularly, and more often if you use it a lot or cook greasy food. If you see a smell, shedding, damage, or it still feels sticky after washing, do not keep using it.
If a scouring pad says antibacterial, do I not need to replace it often?
No. An antibacterial label is the maker's claim, and does not mean it is fine however long you use it. Leftover food and moisture still make a scouring pad dirty. Wringing it out after use, drying it in a ventilated place, and replacing it regularly is the more reliable approach.
Can the same scouring pad be used for both pots and dishes?
We do not recommend it. A rough side that has been used to scrub off burnt-on grease can scratch delicate tableware and cups if you use it on them afterward, and it is also less hygienic. It is better to use separate pieces for pots and dishes, and a bulk pack suits using them in separate areas.
