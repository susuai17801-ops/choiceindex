When you search Shopee Taiwan for a rice cooker, products that all say "6 servings" range from NT$676 to NT$2,879 in what we saw (search results on October 28, 2026). Some are traditional outer-pot cookers that use water in an outer pot, some are microcomputer rice cookers, and each listing states its own energy grade and BSMI number, so it is hard to tell the difference. We checked official Taiwan sources: the Energy Administration requires rice cookers to carry an energy efficiency grade label (thermal efficiency in grades 1 to 5), and the Bureau of Standards, Metrology and Inspection (BSMI) lists rice cookers as products that must be inspected. This guide explains these rules, why "servings" cannot be compared directly, and compares three Shopee products at low, midrange, and high price points in one table.

## First, Understand the Difference: Electric Pot, Rice Cooker, Mini Size

| Name | Features (common description and source) | What to watch for |
|---|---|---|
| Traditional electric pot (outer pot with water) | Common description: you put rice and water in the inner pot and add water to the outer pot, and the heat from the outer pot's water cooks the food, so it can cook rice and also steam and stew. The thermal efficiency formula in the annex of the Energy Administration notice has an item for "mass of distilled water added to the outer pot", which shows that the outer-pot-with-water design is covered by the rice cooker efficiency test. Shopee's specification section has a "rice cooker type" field with a "traditional electric pot" option, filled in by the seller | Check whether an inner lid and a steamer tray are included; a type filled in by the seller is not always accurate, so go by the product description |
| Electronic rice cooker (microcomputer, nonstick inner pot) | Common description: it heats the inner pot directly and often has a microcomputer control, automatic keep-warm, and a preset cooking timer. Listings often say "nonstick inner pot", "automatic keep-warm", or "microcomputer". This is our grouping from listing descriptions, not an official classification | Functions and capacity differ by model, so read each model's description and manual |
| Mini size (a little over 1 liter) | A small model of a little over 1 liter that does not always state servings. Listings often say it suits small families and students. Check the capacity and the rated power consumption | It is small, and when no servings are stated you have to judge whether it is enough |

The Energy Administration's registration form divides heating type into indirect, direct, and IH heating, but the notice does not say which kind of product each means, and it does not separate "電鍋" from "電子鍋" as product names. We could not find an official definition of these two names. The grouping above is our reading of the listings' specification sections and descriptions, not an official classification. This page only explains how to choose and is not product safety advice.

## 5 Things to Check When Choosing a Rice Cooker

| What to check | What to look at | A common problem |
|---|---|---|
| Servings and capacity | Makers write "servings" differently. On Shopee we saw products that all say 10 servings, where one states a maximum rice amount of 1.8L and another a cooking capacity of 5L. These figures do not follow one standard, so you cannot treat them as the same size. We could not find an official definition of "servings". Before you order, look at the capacity, size, and weight on the listing, and think about how many servings you usually cook | Looking only at the words "10 servings" and not at the capacity and size |
| Energy efficiency grade | The Ministry of Economic Affairs notice "Allowable Energy Consumption Standard and Energy Efficiency Grading Label Rules, Methods and Inspection for Rice Cookers" (issued December 24, 2018, in effect from January 1, 2020; point 13 and the label figure amended on May 27, 2025) says: it covers rice cookers that meet CNS 2518 and are on BSMI's list of products that must be inspected; the measured thermal efficiency must be at least 72%; the grading table (Annex 5) is grade 1 at 90.0% or higher, grade 2 at 85.0% or higher but below 90.0%, grade 3 at 80.0% or higher but below 85.0%, grade 4 at 76.0% or higher but below 80.0%, and grade 5 at 72.0% or higher but below 76.0%, so a smaller grade number means higher thermal efficiency. The notice also says the grade label must be attached to a visible place on the front or included in the manual when made or imported, and attached or hung on the front when displayed or sold; when a catalog shows the product in text or a table, it must add the labeled thermal efficiency and the energy grade. We did not find a rule on "below which grade a cooker cannot be sold" | The listing's specification section shows no grade; assuming a smaller grade number means a better cooker (the grade only reflects thermal efficiency, not the cooked rice or the features) |
| Inspection mark and its number | On February 13, 2019, BSMI announced a draft amendment of "Inspection Rules for Rice Cookers That Must Be Inspected". The comparison table attached to it lists rice cookers (including stir-fry pots, stew pots, and electric bowls, limited to a rated voltage of 250V or below) as products that must be inspected, with standards including CNS 60335-1 and CNS 60335-2-13 or 60335-2-15. This document is a draft notice, not the final announcement, and we did not find the current official version. The draft also says that a rice cooker of 10 liters or less that has a rice-cooking function and needs an inner pot must additionally meet CNS 2518's thermal efficiency test and labeling. The same document shows examples of the commodity inspection mark, where the identification number is made of a "series letter" plus a 5-character applicant code (for example R30001). The lookup address for the mark is civil.bsmi.gov.tw/bsmi_pqn/. The specification section of a Shopee listing usually shows a BSMI number too, so check it before you order and save a photo of it | The title says "BSMI certified", but the specification section shows no number |
| Voltage and wattage | Household voltage in Taiwan is 110V. Listings usually state a rated voltage and a rated power consumption, and the rice cookers we saw on Shopee range from 200W to 700W. BSMI's "Electrical Appliance Safety" notice (published September 16, 2025) says an ordinary socket or extension cord has a maximum safe current capacity of only 15 amperes. 15 amperes times 110V is about 1,650 watts. That is our own calculation, not a BSMI figure. Before you order, check the rated power consumption and add up the other appliances on the same socket | A cooker brought from your home country or bought across borders that is labeled 220V; looking only at capacity and not at wattage |
| Warranty and after-sales | Listings usually state a warranty period (the ones we saw were mostly 12 months or 1 year, as stated by the sellers). Before you order, save a screenshot of the warranty scope and return terms, and find out where to send it when it breaks | Not knowing where to send it when it breaks, or not reading what the warranty covers; some listings also say to go by the official website for warranty information, so check there too |

## Before You Order, Check These

- Decide on the size first: look at the capacity, size, and weight on the listing and not only at "servings", and make sure it fits the space you have.
- The specification section should show a BSMI number and an energy efficiency grade. Take a screenshot of both. The product pictures should also include the energy grade label (the hint on Shopee's specification field asks the seller to attach it to the product pictures).
- Look up the number in BSMI's commodity inspection mark lookup system. The navigation bar of the Energy Administration's energy efficiency grading label system has "Approved Products", which lets you check the grade registration. Follow the official systems for the exact steps.
- Confirm that the rated voltage is 110V and check the rated power consumption, and think about whether other appliances share the socket you plan to use.
- For a traditional electric pot, check whether the inner lid and the steamer tray are included; for large items, some listings say delivery is only to the Taiwan main island and to the first floor, so read the shipping notes. Read the warranty and after-sales terms, and when it arrives, check the labels on the unit and the manual again.

## Quick Checklist Before You Buy

- How big is it? → "Servings" is defined differently by each maker, so look at the capacity, size, and weight on the listing
- Is there a BSMI number and an energy efficiency grade? → Both the specification section and the product pictures should show them, so take a screenshot first, then check BSMI's and the Energy Administration's systems
- Is the voltage 110V, and how many watts? → Household voltage in Taiwan is 110V, an ordinary socket or extension cord has a maximum safe current capacity of 15 amperes, and the wattage of the other appliances on the same socket counts too
- Traditional electric pot or rice cooker? → Read the listing's description; for a traditional pot, check whether the inner lid and steamer tray are included
- How many months of warranty, and where does it go for repair? → Save a screenshot of the warranty and return terms first

## Frequently Asked Questions

**What is the difference between a 電鍋 and a 電子鍋?**
Official documents do not define these two names. The Energy Administration's registration form divides heating type into indirect, direct, and IH heating, and the notice does not say which kind of product each means. The common description is that a traditional electric pot heats through the water in its outer pot, while an electronic rice cooker heats the inner pot directly and usually has a microcomputer, keep-warm, and a preset cooking timer. Shopee's "rice cooker type" field is filled in by the seller and is not always accurate. Go by each listing's description and the manual to decide which kind you want.

**Does energy grade 5 mean I should not buy it?**
That is not the way to read it. The Energy Administration's notice says the measured thermal efficiency of a rice cooker must be at least 72%, and grade 5 covers 72.0% or higher but below 76.0%, so grade 5 is the lowest band and is still within the notice's standard; the notice also requires the measured value to be at least 97% of the labeled value. We did not find a rule on "below which grade a cooker cannot be sold", and we do not judge which grade suits you. The grade only reflects thermal efficiency and does not say anything about capacity, features, or how the cooked rice turns out.

**Can I trust the energy grade and BSMI number a listing states?**
Do not rely on the listing alone. The energy grade and the BSMI number on a listing are usually entered by the seller in the specification section, and we did not check them in BSMI's or the Energy Administration's systems. The Energy Administration's notice requires the grade label to be attached to a visible place on the front of the product or included in the manual, and a catalog must show it too; Shopee's specification field also asks the seller to attach the energy label to the product pictures. When it arrives, compare the labels on the unit, the manual, and the listing page.

**Does 10 servings mean cooking rice for 10 people?**
We could not find an official definition of "servings". On Shopee we saw products that all say 10 servings, where one states a maximum rice amount of 1.8L and another a cooking capacity of 5L, so the figures differ and you cannot treat them as the same size. Before you order, look at the capacity, size, and manual stated on each listing.

**Can these three products replace each other?**
Not necessarily. The three differ in capacity, power, energy grade, type, and price (see the table above), and putting them side by side is only for comparison, not a sign that they are interchangeable. Other shops on Shopee also sell rice cookers of the same brands with different models, so check the model, wattage, energy grade, and BSMI number in each listing.
