## In Taiwan, Should You Boil Tap Water Before Drinking?

Taiwan's water utilities do not all say the same thing. The FAQ of Taiwan Water Corporation (台水) says that under the Drinking Water Management Act, tap water must be disinfected with chlorine, so it contains residual chlorine, with a standard of 0.2–1.0 mg per liter; if the chlorine smell bothers you, you can boil it for a few minutes with the lid open, let it stand in an uncovered container for a few hours, or use activated carbon or a water purifier to remove it. Taipei Water Department (北水, which supplies Taipei City and some nearby areas) says the water it supplies meets drinking water quality standards and can be drunk directly, but the water first passes through a building's storage tank and rooftop water tank; if these are not cleaned regularly, the water quality may change, and it recommends cleaning them once a year.

So the point is not only the tap water itself, but also whether the water tank where you live is cleaned regularly. Ask your landlord or employer how often the tank at your dorm or their home is cleaned; when unsure, many people boil water before drinking it. We did not find an official statement covering all of Taiwan on where water must be boiled or can be drunk straight from the tap; follow your local water utility.

## Can an Electric Kettle from Home Be Used?

Look at the label on the base or body first. One marked only 220V–240V uses a voltage different from an ordinary Taiwan socket, so we do not recommend plugging it in directly; a plug adapter only changes the shape of the plug, not the voltage. An electric kettle is a high-power heating appliance, so buying a 110V one in Taiwan is the simplest. For a full explanation of voltage and frequency labels, see our 110V hair dryer guide.

## What to Check When Buying an Electric Kettle Sold in Taiwan

- **Commodity inspection mark** — Under the BSMI's (Bureau of Standards, Metrology and Inspection) principles for determining products subject to inspection (amended April 15, 2024), “electric kettles (電壺)” rated 250V or below with a capacity of 10 liters or less are subject to mandatory inspection, including quick-boil kettles (快煮壺), electric tea kettles, and electric hot water dispensers, under standards such as CNS 60335-1 and CNS 60335-2-15. The seller of our pick lists BSMI number R3C302; we did not verify it.
- **Where the 304 applies** — Many products say “304 stainless steel”, but check whether that means the body, the interior, or the lid. The Taipei Water Department FAQ notes that plastic seals or whistles on a new kettle can give off an odor when exposed to steam, and aging plastic parts can too.
- **Boil-dry protection and automatic shut-off** — Two safety features sellers often list: it does not heat without water, and it switches off when the water boils. We did not test them; when it arrives, check how they work in the manual.
- **Capacity and weight** — For one person, 1–1.2 liters is enough; for several people sharing, look at 1.5–2 liters. A full kettle gets heavy, so think about whether an elderly person or someone with less strength can lift it. This is our own summary, not an official recommendation.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Can you drink tap water straight from the tap in Taiwan?**
It depends on where you are. Taipei Water Department says the water it supplies meets drinking water quality standards and can be drunk directly, but if a building's storage tank and water tank are not cleaned regularly, the quality may change. The Taiwan Water Corporation FAQ we read does not discuss drinking it directly. When unsure, many people boil it first, and you can ask your landlord how often the water tank is cleaned.

**After it boils, should I open the lid and keep boiling for a few minutes?**
The Taiwan Water Corporation FAQ says that if the chlorine smell bothers you, you can boil the water for a few minutes with the lid open to remove residual chlorine. Taipei Water Department, on the other hand, says trihalomethane levels in Taipei's water are very low (an average of 0.007 mg per liter, against a standard of 0.080) and considers boiling with the lid open unnecessary. The two utilities say different things; follow your local water utility for your area.

**What is the white stuff inside my kettle?**
According to Taipei Water Department, it is usually limescale, mainly calcium carbonate, and does not affect health. Clean it as the manual says.
