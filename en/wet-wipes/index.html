How to Choose Wet Wipes (濕紙巾) in Taiwan: Pure Water, Alcohol, Antibacterial, and the Cost per Sheet

Last updated: October 23, 2026

For wiping hands, mouths, tables, and other surfaces when you are out, wet wipes are something almost every home stocks up on. But the shelf is full of wording such as pure water, alcohol, antibacterial, thick, and baby, and a pack can cost anything from a few dollars to dozens. This guide explains the common types, 4 things to check, and how to work out the cost per sheet, so you can decide which one to buy.

First, Understand the 3 Common Types of Wet Wipes

TypeFeaturesWho it is for
Pure water wipesMade with pure water (often ultra-pure or RO water) and nonwoven fabric, and usually labeled "99.9% pure water". Types with no alcohol or fragrance are relatively less irritating, but most still add a small amount of preservative or antibacterial ingredient, so check the ingredient listWiping hands, mouth, and face, and babies and people with sensitive skin (still check the ingredients before use)
Alcohol wipesContain a certain share of alcohol, which evaporates fast, and are used on the surface of things such as tables, handles, and phones. They are not always suitable for dry or sensitive skin or for babies, and alcohol is flammable, so keep them away from fireWiping the surface of things when you are out, and wiping adult hands before a meal
Antibacterial / cleaning wipesContain an antibacterial agent or cleaning agent and are labeled for wiping tables, kitchens, and bathrooms. Most are not recommended for direct contact with the face and mouth, and you should dispose of them as the pack says after useCleaning surfaces at home, in the kitchen, and in the bathroom

No single type is "the best". It depends on whether you are wiping a person or an object, whether there is a baby at home, and whether your skin is sensitive. For wiping the face, the mouth, or a baby's bottom, look first at a pure water type with simple ingredients. Consider alcohol or antibacterial types only for wiping tables and handles. Also, most wet wipes "cannot be flushed down the toilet". If the pack does not say they disperse in water, always put them in the trash, to avoid blocking the pipes.

4 Things to Check When Choosing Wet Wipes

What to checkWhat to look at
Ingredient list and stated useWhether it is pure water, alcohol, or an antibacterial formula, and whether it has fragrance and preservatives. The ingredient list should be clear, and the use should say whether it is for hands, face, or objects. "99.9% pure water" is the maker's figure, and what makes up the remaining 0.1% is what matters
Thickness and nonwoven materialOnes that are too thin tear after one wipe and you need to pull more sheets, which is less of a good deal. Thicker types and spunlace nonwoven are usually more durable and shed less lint
Sheets per pack, and cost per sheetDo not look only at the total price. Work out "total price ÷ number of sheets" for the cost per sheet, and then look at the size of one sheet. For the same pack of 85 sheets, a sheet of 18 × 14 cm and a larger sheet use up very different amounts
Lid, packaging, and expiryWhether there is a re-sealable lid affects whether the wipes dry out after opening. Also check the manufacture date and shelf life, whether the pack is sealed, and try to use it up soon after opening

How to Work Out the Cost per Sheet So the Price per Pack Does Not Fool You

Do not look only at "how much for one pack". Work out "total price ÷ number of sheets". Suppose one pack of 85 sheets costs $20, which is about $0.24 per sheet. Another pack of 80 sheets costs $39, which is about $0.49 per sheet, double the price. Then look at the size of one sheet: even at $0.24 per sheet, a sheet of 18 × 14 cm and a larger sheet need a different number of sheets each time you wipe, so the real cost differs too. Also, some shops list several sizes and price ranges at once, and a price may be a promotional price for a specific size or time. Before you order, confirm the actual price and number of sheets of the exact option you chose, and then work out the cost per sheet, so the figure is accurate.

Choosing by Situation

  • A baby at home, or sensitive skin — Look first at a pure water type with simple ingredients, check whether the ingredient list has fragrance and alcohol, and try a small area the first time.
  • Wiping tables, handles, and phones — Alcohol or antibacterial types remove dirt and evaporate faster, but check whether the material can be wiped (check the instructions first for screens and wooden surfaces).
  • Out and about, always in your bag — Choose a small pack with a lid, which is easy to carry and does not dry out easily. For heavy use at home, a large pack usually has a lower cost per sheet.
  • Stocking up at home — Check the shelf life and whether the pack is intact. Do not stock up more than you can use within the shelf life, and remember to close the pack tightly after opening.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do pure water wipes really contain nothing else?
Not necessarily. "99.9% pure water" is the share the maker states, and the rest is often a preservative or antibacterial agent, used to keep wet wipes from going moldy or breeding bacteria. To know what is really in it, read the full ingredient list on the pack, and do not go only by the slogan on the front.
Can wet wipes be flushed down the toilet?
Most cannot. The fibers of ordinary wet wipes do not break up easily in water, and flushing them can block the pipes. Only products whose pack clearly says they disperse in water can be flushed, and any without that label should go in the trash.
What should I do if wet wipes dry out before I finish the pack?
It is usually because the lid was not closed tightly or the adhesive no longer works. When you buy, choose a type with a re-sealable lid, close it tightly every time, and keep it out of direct sunlight. We do not recommend adding water to wipes that have dried out and using them, because bacteria can grow.
Can wet wipes be used on the face or on a baby?
It depends on the use stated on the product and the ingredients. Only types labeled for the face or for babies are suitable, and we suggest trying a small area first. Alcohol types and antibacterial cleaning types are not recommended for wiping the face directly or for use on babies.
