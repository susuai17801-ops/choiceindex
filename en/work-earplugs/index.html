How to Choose Work Earplugs (工作耳塞) in Taiwan: NRR Noise Reduction, Foam vs Corded or Stemmed, and the Cost per Pair

Last updated: October 24, 2026

If you spend every day around machinery, mowers, drilling, and grinding, or ride a motorcycle to work, your ears are under constant noise, and earplugs are almost the cheapest protection. But on the shop page "noise reduction", "super quiet", "NRR31", and "professional" are written all over, so which number should you look at, and which type is easier to wear? This guide explains the common types, 4 things to check, and how to work out the cost per pair, so you can decide which one to buy.

First, Understand the 3 Common Types of Work Earplugs

TypeFeaturesWho it is for
Roll-down foam earplugsYou roll the foam into a thin cylinder, put it into the ear canal, and it slowly expands to fit. Noise reduction is generally high and the price is low. But you have to roll the foam thin first, and if your hands are dirty or stiff or you do not roll it well, it is hard to seat firmly and you may have to take it out and put it in again and againPeople on a budget who have clean hands and can handle them easily, and who need high noise reduction
Stemmed push-in foam earplugsThe front is a pre-shaped foam tip with a stem behind it, so you push it straight into the ear canal without rolling it first. Many styles can also use a cord so it hangs around your neck when needed. The hardness, shape, and size of the tip differ by style, and not every ear canal fits it exactlyPeople whose hands are often dusty, who wear gloves, or who often put earplugs in and take them out
Silicone / multi-flange reusable earplugsMade of silicone or soft rubber in several umbrella-shaped layers, they can be washed and reused, and the unit price is higher. Noise reduction is usually not as high as top-spec foam, but you do not have to replace them each time, and they are less bothered by dirtPeople who often put them in and take them out, want to reuse them, and are not in an especially noisy place

No single type is "the best". It mainly depends on what environment you are in, how long you wear them, and how easily your hands can handle them. For the highest noise reduction at a low price, foam is usually the first choice. If you often put them in and take them out, stemmed push-in is less trouble, and consider silicone only if you want to reuse them. Also remember that the noise reduction on the label is a number measured in a laboratory under ideal conditions, and when you actually wear them they do not always seat as firmly, so how much they really reduce depends on whether you insert them correctly and firmly. If you work in a high-noise environment for a long time, please follow your workplace's occupational safety rules and ask a professional. This page only explains how to choose and is not occupational safety or medical advice.

4 Things to Check When Choosing Work Earplugs

What to checkWhat to look at
Noise reduction (NRR) and where the figure comes fromNRR (Noise Reduction Rating) is the noise reduction level used in the United States. The higher the number, the more noise reduction is stated, for example NRR 31. But this is a value under laboratory conditions, and "certified by an authoritative laboratory" on a shop page is mostly the seller's label, so before ordering it is best to see whether it comes with the test body and method. In real use the noise reduction is usually lower than the stated value, so do not worry too much about a difference of one or two points, and fitting them correctly matters more
How you wear them and how they fit your ear canalFirst decide whether you want roll-down or stemmed push-in, and then see whether the shape and size suit your ear canal. Ear canals differ in size, and the same earplug is just right for one person and too tight or too loose for another. If they are not seated firmly, the noise reduction drops a lot. The first time, you can buy a small amount to try, and see whether they ache or feel swollen after a long time
Use and limitsFirst read what the seller says it is for. Some say "for use at work and study, not for use during sleep", and then you should not use them for sleeping. In places where you need to hear alarms, horns, and colleagues calling (for example riding a motorcycle or roadside work), too much noise reduction can be dangerous, so judge whether you can accept it. There is a swallowing risk for children and young children, and the seller also reminds you not to give them to children
Cost per pair and how often you replace themDo not look only at the unit price. Work out "total price ÷ number of pairs", and then estimate how many wears before you replace them. Foam earplugs get dirty and out of shape after one or two uses, and are usually for one-time or short-term use. Silicone types can be washed and reused, and cost more each, but the average cost is not necessarily high. A shop often has multi-buy offers and a minimum order amount, and you should count those in too

How to Work Out the Cost per Pair So a Low Unit Price Does Not Fool You

Do not look only at "how much for one pair". Look at "total price ÷ number of pairs", and then work out how many uses you really get. As an example of the calculation: if you replace a foam earplug every two or three days, you use 10 to 15 pairs a month, and the monthly cost is "cost per pair × 10 to 15". Another reusable silicone earplug costs more each, but if you can wash it and use it for two or three months, the average per month may actually be cheaper. This is only an example of how to calculate, not a guarantee, and the real life depends on the environment, how you clean them, and personal habits. A shop may list different colors, counts, and combinations at once, and a price is for a specific option, so before ordering check whether the option you chose is "one pair" or "several pairs", and then work out the cost per pair so the figure is accurate.

Choosing by Situation

  • Constant high noise: factory, grinding, and drilling — Look first at a high-noise-reduction foam type that you can seat firmly, and make sure you can handle the way it is worn. If you wear gloves or your hands are often dusty, stemmed push-in is easier. In this kind of place, go by your workplace's rules and the advice of professionals.
  • Farm machinery, mowers, and chainsaws outdoors — For long work, check whether they get sore or swollen when worn for a long time and whether they loosen with sweat. If you need to hear what is around you, judge whether the noise reduction is too much.
  • Commuting by motorcycle — Wind noise and engine noise building up over time can also hurt your ears, but fully blocking your ears means you cannot hear horns or cars behind you, so first judge whether you can accept that, or choose a gentler noise reduction, and do not take risks for the sake of quiet.
  • Studying, flying, and travel — You only want to reduce distraction, so you may not need high noise reduction, and comfort matters more. Earplugs for sleeping differ in shape and material, so if a work earplug says it is not for sleeping, do not use it to sleep.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is NRR, and is a higher number always better?
NRR (Noise Reduction Rating) is the level used in the United States for how much noise an earplug reduces, and a higher number means more reduction is stated. But it is a value measured in a laboratory with ideal fitting, and in real use the noise reduction is usually lower than the stated value because the earplug is not seated firmly enough or does not fit the ear canal. So you do not need to worry too much about a difference of one or two points, and inserting them correctly and firmly matters more.
How do I wear foam earplugs so they work?
The usual advice is to wash your hands clean, roll the foam into a thin cylinder, use your other hand to pull the ear back and up, push the earplug into the ear canal, and hold it with a finger for a few seconds while it expands and fits. For stemmed push-in types, push straight in and turn slightly until it fits. If the sounds around you clearly become muffled after you put them in, they are usually seated firmly. The way to wear each product differs a little, so go by the seller and the pack instructions.
Can I use them for sleeping or give them to children?
We do not recommend it. For this earplug, the seller says "for use at work and study, not for use during sleep", and reminds you not to give it to children and young children, to prevent swallowing. Earplugs meant for sleeping usually differ in shape, material, and noise reduction design, so we suggest choosing a sleep style separately.
Can earplugs be reused, and how often should I replace them?
There is no fixed answer. Foam earplugs absorb sweat and dirt easily, and the usual advice is to replace them according to how dirty or out of shape they are, and if the pack says single use, do not reuse them. Silicone types can be washed as the seller says and reused. If an earplug is out of shape, hardened, smells, or your ear feels uncomfortable, replace it, and if your ears keep feeling uncomfortable, ask a professional.
